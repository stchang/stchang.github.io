An operation that programmers commonly want to do is to take the substring of some string.

Assume we have some function \mathrm{SUB}: \textsf{Language}\times\textsf{NatNum}\rightarrow\textsf{Language}

For example \mathrm{SUB}(\{\texttt{nerds},\texttt{kitkat},\texttt{skittles}\},3)=\{\texttt{ner},\texttt{kit},\texttt{ski}\}

Prove the following statement

IF L IS-A Regular Language THEN \mathrm{SUB}(L,4) IS-A Regular Language

Make sure the proof has all the usual required components and uses correct constructors and follows correct proof syntax from this course.

2 Proving That a Language is Not a Regular Language🔗

We saw in the basic proof by induction lecture examples, and in the last homework, that we occasionally needed to prove "REFL" statements. That is, statements that are True due to the reflexivity of equality.

If we have alphabet \Sigma = \left\{\mathtt{1},\mathtt{0},\mathtt{=},\mathtt{x},\mathtt{y},\mathtt{+}\right\}

  1. give 3 strings, made from the alphabet characters, that are True "REFL" statements (if the numbers are interpreted as numbers, operators have their typical meaning, and other characters represent variables)

  2. give 3 strings, made from the alphabet characters, that are False "REFL" statements

  3. if the set of all True "REFL" statements made from characters in \Sigma is called REFL, prove

    not (REFL IS-A Regular Language)

For parts 1 and 2, you may post specific examples to Piazza and ask for confirmation about your understanding of the problem.

For Part 3, use the Pumping Lemma and proof by contradiction. Make sure your proof has all the correct components and use the proper constructors. Remember that a proof by contradiction begins with assuming the opposite of the statement to prove and then proceeds like any normal proof, EXCEPT the proof must end in the statement Contradiction! that is justified with a proof of some statement X \,\mathrm{AND}\, \textbf{not} X This contradiction allows the conclusion that the assumption must be False and that the original statement to prove is True.

More specifically, the contradictory statements must be s IS-Pumpable and not (s IS-Pumpable) where IS-Pumpable is defined by \mathrm{ThePumpingLemma} and s is a counterexample that you must carefully choose that is in the language REFL and longer than some "pumping length" p, also specified by \mathrm{ThePumpingLemma}.

3 CFGs and Derivations🔗

Here’s a context-free grammar (CFG), called \mathit{PY}_{420}, representing the core of a Python-like language:

\left\langle STMTS\right\rangle

\rightarrow

\left\langle STMT\right\rangle; \left\langle STMTS\right\rangle \mid \left\langle STMT\right\rangle

\left\langle STMT\right\rangle

\rightarrow

\left\langle ID\right\rangle \texttt{=} \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle

\mid \texttt{if} \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle\!\!\texttt{:} \left\langle STMT\right\rangle \texttt{elif} \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle\!\!\texttt{:} \left\langle STMT\right\rangle \texttt{else:} \left\langle STMT\right\rangle

\mid \texttt{println(} \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle \texttt{)} \mid \left\langle DEFFN\right\rangle \mid \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle

\left\langle DEFFN\right\rangle

\rightarrow

\texttt{def} \left\langle ID\right\rangle \texttt{(}\left\langle IDS\right\rangle \texttt{)}\texttt{:} \left\langle STMT\right\rangle

\left\langle EXPR\right\rangle

\rightarrow

\left\langle ID\right\rangle \mid \left\langle NUM\right\rangle \mid \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle \texttt{==} \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle \mid \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle \texttt{-} \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle

\mid \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle \texttt{/} \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle \mid \left\langle FUN\right\rangle \mid \left\langle ID\right\rangle\texttt{(} \left\langle EXPRS\right\rangle \texttt{)}

\left\langle FUN\right\rangle

\rightarrow

\texttt{fn} \left\langle IDS\right\rangle \!\!\texttt{:} \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle

\left\langle EXPRS\right\rangle

\rightarrow

\left\langle EXPR\right\rangle, \left\langle EXPRS\right\rangle \mid \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle

\left\langle NUM\right\rangle

\rightarrow

0\mid 1\mid 2\mid 3\mid 4\mid 5\mid 6\mid 7\mid 8\mid 9

\left\langle IDS\right\rangle

\rightarrow

\left\langle ID\right\rangle \texttt{,} \left\langle IDS\right\rangle \mid \left\langle ID\right\rangle

\left\langle ID\right\rangle

\rightarrow

\texttt{v}\mid\texttt{w}\mid\texttt{x}\mid\texttt{y}\mid\texttt{z}

(Yes, real-world languages are much more complicated than textbook examples.)

The variables (nonterminals) of the CFG are all the names enclosed in angle brackets, e.g., \left\langle EXPR\right\rangle is a variable name. All other symbols used in the rules are terminals (ignoring whitespace) (also, you may treat multi-symbol terminals as one terminal, e.g., \texttt{fn}).

  1. Give two strings in \mathit{PY}_{420}’s language by showing their derivation steps. Each given string must have at least six derivation steps and each derivation should use (mostly) different rules.

  2. Give a formal description, i.e., with a constructor, of the grammar \mathit{PY}_{420}. You may assume that the given rules are in a set called \mathit{PY420RULES}.

  3. Give parse trees for the following strings in the language of \mathit{PY}_{420}:

    1. \texttt{def w(x,y): x / x - y / y}

    2. \texttt{v = fn x,y: x - y; def w(x,y): v(x,y)-1}

    3. \texttt{if x == y: 1 elif v == w: 2 else: 3}
